ZCASH AT A CROSSROADS: THE CASE FOR ENDING THE DEV FUND

A proposal to end Zcash's Development Fund early opens a debate over decentralization, miner rewards and the next chapter for ZEC.
By John Doe
OCT 30, 2026 // ZCASH // GOVERNANCE

Zcash is confronting a question few cryptocurrency projects willingly ask: when should a development subsidy end? Josh Swihart, a prominent Zcash ecosystem figure who helped advance the current funding model, is calling for the Development Fund to run its scheduled course through November 2028—or be terminated in its entirety, rather than selectively dismantled.

His intervention is not a rejection of Zcash. It is an argument about what a mature, decentralized monetary network should become.

THE PROPOSAL: ALL OR NOTHING

The debate centers on proposals to eliminate the Zcash Community Grants (ZCG) allocation before the existing funding arrangement expires, while retaining the Coinholder-controlled portion. Swihart argues that preserving one part of the structure while removing another would leave the underlying concerns unresolved.

His position is clear: honor the existing Development Fund until its scheduled November 2028 expiration, or make the case for ending the whole mechanism earlier. This is a proposal and public argument—not an enacted protocol change.

WHY THIS COULD BE BULLISH FOR ZEC

A predictable, finite funding mechanism can support development during a network's formative years. But permanent or concentrated funding can also raise difficult questions about incentives, accountability and who ultimately controls a supposedly decentralized project.

Ending the Development Fund would remove its designated share of future block rewards, potentially directing more newly issued ZEC to miners under the applicable consensus rules. It would not erase the supply already issued, nor would it automatically change Zcash's 21-million-coin maximum supply.

For supporters of a more market-driven Zcash, the appeal is straightforward: fewer protocol-level funding allocations, stronger emphasis on independent development and a monetary system less reliant on privileged recipients. That is a possible long-term investment thesis, not a guarantee of higher prices.

THE TRADE-OFF NOBODY SHOULD IGNORE

Development is not free. Shielded transactions, wallet infrastructure, security reviews and protocol upgrades require sustained engineering work. Removing a dedicated funding stream could force teams to rely more heavily on donations, independent financing and market-based business models.

Those alternatives may improve accountability—or create new funding gaps. The outcome depends on the transition, not merely the slogan.

ZEROTRACE // ANALYSIS

Zcash's strongest bullish case is not that every subsidy must disappear overnight. It is that a privacy network can eventually stand on its own: competitive miners, independent builders, transparent incentives and privacy technology people actually use.

WHAT HAPPENS NEXT?

The Development Fund does not end because one influential participant calls for it. Any change would require the relevant governance and protocol processes. The existing November 2028 horizon remains central to the debate unless a different arrangement is formally adopted.

Swihart's proposal places a larger question on the table: should Zcash's next chapter be defined by managed funding—or by a stronger commitment to decentralization and open competition?

For $ZEC, the answer could shape the network's incentives long after today's market cycle has passed.
